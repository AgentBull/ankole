# Analogical Foresight

A transfer claim is the core unit of analogical analysis:

`supported source-case mechanism -> specific target mapping -> conditions
required for transfer -> target-side observation`

Historical cases are useful when this chain exposes a mechanism, a missing
variable, a possible path, a boundary condition, or a question to investigate
in the target. Surface similarity and a persuasive historical story do not
establish that the same mechanism operates in the target.

## Frame the target without an analogy

State the target question, information cutoff, horizon, and current stage.
First collect enough target information to understand the subject. Then sketch
the target structure before you name historical cases. This limits the chance
that a vivid or familiar case will define the problem before the target is
understood.

Describe the target in terms that help causal comparison:

- observed preconditions, event sequences, constraints, and outcomes so far;
- inferred causal relations and the evidence for each inference; and
- unknown factors, uncertain relations, or unexplained stages that could change
  the analysis.

The roles in a causal structure depend on the question. Use role names only
when they clarify the mapping. Similar sequences can have different causes, and
a coherent narrative can hide chance, institutional constraints, policy
conflict, implementation failure, or unintended effects.

State which cutoff applies to target information and to information about the
historical cases. In a reconstructed forecast, later target events cannot
select the cases, define the target structure, or judge the mappings. If the
task requires a strict as-of reconstruction, use only information that was
knowable by that cutoff.

The target frame is ready when it can stand without a named analogy, observed
conditions and sequences are identified as observations, and every important
causal relation is marked as inferred or open with its evidential basis.

## Generate candidates from causal gaps

Use the uncertain relations, missing factors, and unexplained stages in the
target frame as search directions. Search for the same directed relation,
causal dynamic, or functional constraint across different actors, periods, and
domains. Generate candidates before you evaluate them so that the first
familiar case does not end the search.

Use unexplained causal roles, relations, stages, and failed mappings to direct
later searches. When the candidate pool stays inside one familiar domain or
repeats one historical story, you can ask a context-isolated subagent that sees
only the target frame to propose cross-domain cases, failed cases, and cases
with reversed outcomes. This subagent generates candidates; it does not judge
the target.

There is no required candidate count or search-round count.

## Establish the source-case mechanism

Verify the facts of each retained case and identify what evidence supports the
claimed mechanism. An event sequence is not yet a causal explanation. Check
whether a common cause, an alternative mechanism, chance, selection effects,
or a later narrative can explain the same sequence.

Map the source case by its relevant preconditions, sequence, proposed causal
direction, factor roles, stage, duration, scale, scope, and outcome. A case can
be useful for one relation or stage without matching the whole target. State
the local scope of that analogy and the point at which it stops.

An alias, a subevent or superset, a generic category, or another description of
the same episode cannot provide independent support. It can still supply facts
about the same event. A reversed relation, failed transfer, different stage, or
broken boundary condition can remain useful as a counterexample or as evidence
of the mechanism's limits.

## Audit each transfer claim

Keep the following parts together for every analogy-derived claim that can
affect the answer:

- **Source mechanism:** the mechanism claimed in the historical case and the
  evidence that supports it.
- **Target mapping:** the specific target relation, factor, or stage to which
  the mechanism is mapped.
- **Material differences:** mismatched preconditions, roles, directions,
  timing, scale, scope, actor incentives, or other boundary conditions.
- **Transfer assumptions:** the conditions that must hold for the source
  mechanism to remain relevant in the target.
- **Dependence:** shared sources, nested events, common shocks, policy copying,
  causal propagation, common institutions, or common measurement that link
  this case to other cases.
- **Target-side test:** an observation in the target that could support or
  weaken the proposed transfer.

For behavior by an actor or institution, use that actor's own goals,
constraints, incentives, information, and decision process. Your own expected
behavior in the same position is not evidence about that actor.

Treat material disanalogies as part of the transfer claim. A retained case
should contribute at least one useful result: a candidate mechanism, missing
variable, conditional path, target-side indicator, counterexample, or boundary
condition. Remove a case that contributes only a historical narrative.

## Synthesize across cases

One case can suggest a hypothesis. Several independently informative cases can
reduce reliance on one historical story when they show a comparable mechanism
under comparable boundary conditions. They do not by themselves show that the
mechanism operates in the target.

Examine whether apparent repetition comes from the same episode, institution,
source narrative, underlying shock, propagation path, policy diffusion, or
measurement method. When a positive pattern matters, also examine relevant
cases in which the proposed factor was present but the outcome was absent, or
the outcome occurred through another mechanism.

A purposefully selected analogy set is not a reference class, so its case count
is not a base rate, probability, confidence score, or posterior. A separate
reference-class analysis needs a defined sampling frame and relevant comparison
cases.

Each case can cover only part of the target structure. Evidence that different
relations appear in different cases does not establish that those relations
operate together in the target. State hidden factors, transferred mechanisms,
paths, and scenarios as conditional claims and compare them with direct target
evidence.

An analogy can propose an ACH hypothesis or identify evidence to seek. A
historical case is not a direct observation of the target; it supports a target
claim only through an explicit and defensible transfer claim.

## Completion criterion

Analogical analysis is complete only when every analogy-derived claim that
affects the answer identifies the source mechanism and the evidence that
distinguishes it from material alternative explanations or temporal
succession; the specific relation mapped to the target and its direction,
stage, and scope; the material differences, dependencies, and conditions
required for transfer; and a target-side observation that could support or
weaken the claim.

Account for every discovered counterexample or failed mapping that could
materially change the assessment. The search must also address the material
causal gaps in the target frame and seek plausible cases with failed, absent,
or reversed outcomes. If no candidate can satisfy the transfer chain, conclude
that no defensible analogy was found.
