# Posters and Infographics

## Frame the Assignment

Identify what the composition should make readers see, understand, remember, or do. Resolve the audience, viewing distance, display medium, reading time, canvas, and output format before arranging content.

State the core message in one sentence. Identify the relationship that carries it: time, space, process, comparison, hierarchy, object structure, or atmosphere. Choose one dominant visual object or relationship and let the rest of the page support it.

## Reference Structures

### Exhibition flyer

Build three reading distances into one field. At a distance, one image, object, title, or compositional gesture establishes the subject. At middle distance, the exhibition or event name and central idea become clear. At close range, date, venue, participants, program, and other practical details form a compact and ordered information band.

### General graphic poster

Create one decisive relationship between type, image, object, and geometric form. Let secondary information occupy an edge, narrow index, or local annotation layer. Keep the primary relationship recognizable at thumbnail size, while close reading reveals the factual structure without turning the page into a dashboard.

## Choose the Canvas and Reading Order

Use the user's specified size, orientation, or ratio. When it is not specified, choose from the use case, information volume, primary reading direction, and relationship structure. Compose directly for the selected canvas.

| Common ratio | Common use |
|---|---|
| 9:16 portrait | Mobile reading and vertical narrative |
| 3:4 portrait | Print-like posters and object-led compositions |
| 1:1 square | Social posts and centered structures |
| 4:3 landscape | Dense text-image compositions and document embedding |
| 16:9 landscape | Screens and horizontal relationships |

For a few-page composition, give each page one role. A useful sequence is visual hook, explanation or relationship, then practical action. Keep one shared axis or motif across the pages.

## Match the Graphic to the Relationship

| Core relationship | Useful expression |
|---|---|
| Time and stage changes | Timeline, milestones, or before-and-after states |
| Paths, propagation, and migration | Map, route, flow, or network |
| Steps, mechanisms, and methods | Process, swimlane, or input-process-output structure |
| Differences between objects or views | Juxtaposition, matrix, or scale axis |
| Hierarchy, composition, and systems | Tree, architecture, or layered section |
| Physical objects and materials | Object annotation, local zoom, or visual anatomy |
| Multiple facts or samples | Image matrix, taxonomy map, or data field |
| Emotion, ideas, and attitude | One strong image, type composition, or abstract form |

Use a structure diagram when the relationship itself is central. Give it enough area to become part of the composition, and keep nodes, interfaces, direction, grouping, scale, and legends clear.

## Composition Intent

Before building, record a short intent that states:

- the audience and desired response;
- the dominant visual object or relationship;
- the order in which the eye should move;
- what remains visible at distant, middle, and close reading ranges;
- the one compositional device that holds the page together.

Choose imagery with a clear subject, useful crop space, and a real relation to the message. Let important imagery or objects occupy enough area to affect the whole composition. Anchor labels, routes, dimensions, and local details directly to the parts they explain.

## Editable Delivery and Review

Keep text, shapes, connectors, data labels, and legends editable. When the requested final output is an image, complete the editable PPTX first and then render it.

Before delivery, inspect the composition at thumbnail size and at its intended reading size. Confirm that the subject, main message, and reading path remain clear, and that body text, labels, and legends remain readable in the actual output setting.
