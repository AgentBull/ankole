# Academic Research

## Narrative

Let the audience understand the research value within the first minute. Build the sequence from research context to the research question, method, findings, interpretation, limitations, and contribution. Short talks can compress the context, but the research question and contribution must remain easy to locate.

Use section transitions only when the argument changes phase. Keep a small progress marker, section name, or other stable navigation cue when the deck is long enough to need one.

Build each result page around one conclusion. Place the figure, table, equation, archive, or other research object that supports it in the visual center. Keep its caption, conditions, units, and supplied qualifications close to it.

## Field-Specific Page Focus

- Basic research and theoretical derivation: make definitions, propositions, equations, and proof steps the main objects.
- Computer science and engineering: make system diagrams, code, run traces, ablation results, and performance curves the page skeleton.
- Experimental research: keep the figure, image, or table close to the conclusion that it supports.
- Empirical analysis: combine the main quantitative result with enough narrative context to explain what it means.
- Humanities, architecture, and arts: organize archives, maps, manuscripts, image details, and chronology as research material rather than decoration.

## Reference Structures

### Formal research defense

Use a stable research hierarchy across the deck. Keep title, figure, caption, footnote, section marker, and page number on fixed axes. Let the main research object dominate each result page, with the conclusion and interpretation placed beside it. Use the same coordinates when a sequence expands one method or result across several pages so that changes are easy to compare.

A typical sequence is:

1. Research context and the unresolved question
2. Exact question, hypothesis, or proposition
3. Method, system, corpus, or experiment
4. Major results, one conclusion per page
5. Interpretation and limitations
6. Contribution and direct answer to the research question

### Large-room research talk

Use one message and one main object per page. Reduce local detail to what can be read at distance, then distribute a complex argument across several pages with stable coordinates. Alternate a concise explanation page with a figure-led page when the audience needs orientation before reading the result.

## Page Grammar

- **Question page:** state the exact question and show the scope, key definitions, or gap that makes it necessary.
- **Method page:** show the complete mechanism or experimental design before expanding individual stages.
- **Derivation page:** keep assumptions, symbols, steps, and the local conclusion in one reading path.
- **Result page:** use a conclusion title, one dominant figure or table, direct annotations, and a nearby interpretation.
- **Comparison page:** keep baselines, variants, and evaluation conditions on the same scale.
- **Limitations page:** separate established findings, uncertainty, and the next unresolved question.
- **Closing page:** answer the research question and state the contribution in the same terms used near the start.

## Density and Review

Keep academic pages full enough to carry a complete local argument, while preserving readable type and clear separation between the main object, explanation, and notes. Split crowded content across pages before compressing captions, equations, or labels beyond their useful reading size.

Before delivery, confirm that the sequence preserves every required definition, condition, qualification, number, label, and relationship from the supplied material. Check that each figure or table has a clear role and that the title sequence alone tells the research story.
