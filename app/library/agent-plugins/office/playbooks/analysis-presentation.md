# Analysis and Decision

## Narrative

Treat the deck as a reading artifact unless it is explicitly for a live presentation. Let a reader understand the overall judgment first, then follow the mechanism and evidence that support it.

Use a declarative title when the page supports a clear judgment. Build support from the title to the main chart, table, or structural diagram, then to direct annotations, interpretation, and supplied notes. Keep recommendations after the analysis that justifies them.

## Reference Structures

### Policy briefing chart pack

Build each page from a conclusion title, one main chart, direct labels on the important values, and a compact note for source, scenario, or uncertainty. Use stable chart coordinates and annotation positions so that the audience can compare consecutive pages quickly. Distinguish observed values, forecasts, and alternative scenarios through consistent line or fill semantics.

### Annual analytical report

Open with a small set of takeaways. Move from the current situation to the mechanism that explains it, then to evidence, risks, scenarios, and practical implications. Use section summaries to reconnect detailed pages to the main judgment before the argument moves to the next theme.

## Page Grammar

- **Executive summary:** state the main judgment, its strongest support, the largest uncertainty, and the decision that follows.
- **Trend page:** use one primary time series or a coordinated group of charts, with inflection points and key values labeled directly.
- **Mechanism page:** make causality, sequence, feedback, or dependency visible with a structural diagram.
- **Comparison page:** compare options, regions, cohorts, or periods on the same dimensions and scale.
- **Scenario page:** keep the baseline, upside, downside, assumptions, and sensitivity in one coordinate system.
- **Risk page:** connect each risk to its trigger, exposure, possible effect, and response.
- **Decision page:** state the recommendation, the conditions that support it, and the signals that would change it.

## Charts, Tables, and Interpretation

Charts carry complete axes, units, legends, and supplied notes so that they can stand on their own. Label key totals, inflection points, gaps, and growth rates directly beside the relevant marks. Place interpretation next to the visual object instead of separating it into a distant paragraph.

Tables use stable columns and comparable units. Align numbers consistently, keep definitions near the table, and provide an interpretation zone when the reader needs help to find the decisive pattern.

## Density and Review

Keep body pages compact, with charts, tables, and diagrams as the main argument. Use the cover, section transitions, and final decision page as measured pauses. Keep title, main evidence, interpretation, and notes in stable zones across related pages.

Before delivery, read only the page titles and confirm that they form a complete analytical argument. Then check every chart and table against the supplied labels, values, units, time periods, scenarios, and qualifications.
